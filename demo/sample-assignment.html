Week 6 Reading: How Do We Know What We Know?

Due Friday by 11:59pm Points 25 Submitting a text entry box

Epistemology is the branch of philosophy that investigates knowledge itself: what it is, where it comes from, and how far it reaches. Before you write your response, read the passage below carefully. You may want to read it through twice, since the second half builds on a distinction that is easy to miss the first time.

1. Justification and the scientific method

Most philosophers accept that knowing something requires more than believing it. A belief can be true by accident, and an accidental truth is not knowledge. The traditional analysis therefore adds a third requirement, justification: a person knows a claim only when the claim is true, they believe it, and their belief rests on adequate evidence. The principal difficulty is deciding what counts as adequate. Whether a thermometer reading justifies a belief about the weather depends on the instrument, the observer, and the background assumptions that precede the observation.

The scientific method is often described as a procedure, but it is better understood as a discipline of doubt. A researcher proposes a hypothesis, derives a prediction, and then tries, sincerely, to show that the prediction fails. Karl Popper argued that this willingness to be refuted is the principle that separates science from pseudoscience. A theory that could not possibly be contradicted by any observation does not affect our expectations at all, and so, he concluded, it has no empirical effect and tells us nothing about the world.

Their experiments were thorough, though the results passed through three laboratories before anyone thought to cite the original site of the measurements.

Thomas Kuhn complicated this picture. In his account, scientists rarely abandon a framework because of a single failed prediction. Instead they adapt the framework, adopt auxiliary assumptions, and proceed as before, until the anomalies accumulate and a revolution replaces the whole paradigm. Kuhn did not mean to imply that science is irrational; critics were too quick to infer that. His point was that evidence is always interpreted from within a perspective, and that the choice between perspectives cannot be settled by evidence alone.

2. Discrete observations, continuous theories

An experiment produces a finite set of discrete measurements, yet a theory makes claims about indefinitely many cases. No quantity of observations can logically guarantee a universal law, a problem David Hume identified in the eighteenth century and that still lacks a fully satisfying solution. Statisticians respond with probability: rather than proving a hypothesis, they estimate how surprising the data would be if the hypothesis were false. This shift is subtle, and it is where many interpretive mistakes arise, particularly the confusion between correlation and causal explanation.
